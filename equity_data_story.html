Part I

Where is the data missing — and why it matters

Before any analysis, the data must be audited. The CPE dataset contains 80+ unique CSV files (after MD5 deduplication): 12 policing event files and 60+ American Community Survey (ACS) Census tables covering poverty, income, employment, education, and housing for each department. The clustering pipeline uses the 12 policing files; the ACS tables could not be joined because the incident records carry no census tract identifier. Across these policing files, key demographic fields — race, gender, incident reason, and geographic coordinates — are missing at very different rates depending on the type of policing record. This is not random loss; it reflects departmental recording practices. Vehicle stops record race and gender reliably, while use-of-force and incident reports often omit them entirely.

Missing rates (%) for six key fields across source files. Blue = complete, red = entirely missing. Pattern is structural, not random.
Why this matters: Missingness that depends on incident type violates the Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) assumption. Imputing these fields without acknowledging the pattern would inject bias. The pipeline addresses this by adding binary missingness flags as features, so that the clustering algorithm can detect recording gaps as a structural pattern — which it does (Cluster C6).
Race categories recorded per source file. Minneapolis UoF has no Hispanic category. Los Angeles Incidents, Los Angeles Reports, and Indianapolis UoF (415,278 records combined) have no race column at all.
Blind spot: Hispanic subjects are invisible in the Minneapolis use-of-force file (25,801 records). Asian subjects appear in most files but at very low rates. Any equity conclusion about these groups is limited to departments that actually record them.
Why do some bubbles look dimmer? Departments shown with reduced opacity (like San Francisco) have policing records that do not include a race column, limiting their value for equity analysis. They still enter the clustering pipeline and contribute to the overall pattern detection, but they cannot be used for racial disparity calculations. Indianapolis had a column typo (SUBJECT_RACT instead of SUBJECT_RACE) that has been corrected. Departments marked "TBD" are present in the dataset but their record counts have not yet been individually verified. Together the dataset spans use of force, vehicle stops, field interviews, arrests, and incident reports across 12 departments in 9 states.
Part II

The cities behind the numbers

The departments in this dataset span cities with very different socioeconomic conditions. These indicators provide context for the policing patterns identified in later sections. The Census socioeconomic tables included in the dataset (poverty, income, employment, education, housing) are available at census-tract level and can be joined to policing records where geographic identifiers exist.

Source: US Census Bureau QuickFacts (2020). The CPE dataset also includes Census tract-level socioeconomic tables for each department, not used here because incident records lack a geographic join key.
What the numbers show: Los Angeles has the highest peak racial disparity (3.8×) with a 17.0% poverty rate. Minneapolis has 19.1% poverty and contributes 51% of all records. Dallas has 17.5% poverty and is the only city recording officer race. Austin has the lowest poverty rate (12.6%) and the smallest sample (131 records). The dataset includes Census socioeconomic tables (poverty, income, employment, education, housing) for each department, which were not integrated into the clustering because the incident records lack a geographic identifier for a row-level join.
Part III

The gap between population and policing

If policing were equitable, each racial group's share of police encounters would roughly match their share of the city's population. It doesn't. The dumbbell chart below shows the gap for all five groups in Minneapolis: hollow dots are Census shares, filled dots are policing incident shares, and the connecting line is the disparity.

Source: CPE dataset (Minneapolis vehicle stops + UoF) · US Census 2020. Dumbbell chart: position encoding on a common scale.
Key finding: The most over-represented group appears in policing records at 1.84× its Census population share. The most under-represented group appears at 0.74× its share. The asymmetry is clear across all five groups.
Part IV

From 87 columns to a usable dataset

The policing event files contain 109 unique columns — far too many for clustering. After removing structurally absent fields (see Part I) and engineering numeric features, 11 survived. Principal Component Analysis then compressed these into 7 components, retaining 95.1% of the variance (4.9% loss). This step is essential: without it, the clustering algorithm would be overwhelmed by redundant and correlated features.

Left: individual explained variance per component. Right: cumulative curve with 95% threshold.

MDS and LLE were also tested but rejected: MDS produced a stress of 533,961 (too high for faithful 2D projection), and LLE fragmented the data into disconnected islands due to the categorical feature boundaries.

Part V

How many distinct policing patterns exist?

Rather than assuming a number of groups, the algorithm was asked to find the natural structure. Five independent statistical criteria were tested for values of k from 3 to 7. The majority converged on k=7 — 7 distinct policing patterns.

Five k-selection methods applied to k=3..7. Bottom-right: vote tally. Consensus at k=7.
Why not just the elbow? The elbow method only measures cohesion (inertia), not separability. Silhouette and Davies-Bouldin measure both. Using all five guards against any single method's blind spots.
Part VI

Can the algorithm reliably separate policing patterns?

Four clustering methods were tested. k-Means produced the most coherent groupings according to the composite rank across Silhouette, Davies-Bouldin, and Calinski-Harabasz. k-Means, GMM, and Agglomerative all assign 100% of records to a cluster. DBSCAN does not — it labels low-density records as “noise” (unclassified), which means incidents are lost from the analysis. For a municipal equity audit where every policing encounter must be categorised, this makes DBSCAN unsuitable for this dataset. DBSCAN also over-segmented the binary feature space into 28 micro-clusters, far too many for actionable policy.

Three standard validity metrics compared across four algorithms. Higher Silhouette and CH indicate tighter clusters; lower DB indicates better separation.
Confidence check: A Gaussian Mixture Model overlay confirmed that 100.0% of incidents were assigned to their cluster with over 80% posterior probability — the groupings reflect genuine structure in the data, not noise. (Note: with binary missingness flags, high confidence is expected.).

Part IX

Does officer race change the outcome?

Only Dallas records officer race alongside subject race — 2,383 use-of-force incidents where both are known. If racial bias were primarily interpersonal (individual officers targeting other races), then Black officers should use force on Black subjects less often than White officers do. The data tells a different story.

Dallas UoF 2016. Grouped bars show what percentage of each officer group's force incidents involve each subject race. If officer race drove outcomes, the bars would differ sharply across groups.
Key finding: White officers use force on Black subjects 57.6% of the time. Black officers use force on Black subjects 58.9% of the time — virtually identical. The pattern holds regardless of who wears the badge, pointing to structural deployment patterns rather than individual bias. The police force itself is 62% White in a city that is 29% White.
Part X

Did policing change over time?

The Minneapolis data spans 2001 to 2018, covering three presidencies. The two panels below track the Black disparity index year by year: how many times more likely a Black resident was to experience policing contact relative to their share of the city population (19.0%).

Minneapolis policing data. Left: use of force (N=25,801, 2008–2018). Right: vehicle stops (N=710,472, 2001–2017). Dashed line = population parity (1.0×). Shaded bands indicate presidential terms. Source: CPE dataset.
What the numbers show: Use-of-force disparity was stable at 3.3× across all three presidencies (Bush: 3.36×, Obama: 3.34×, Trump: 3.15×). Vehicle-stop disparity rose from 1.3× in 2001 to a peak of 2.2× in 2009, then declined to 1.7× by 2017. The pattern did not spike under any single administration. The stability of the use-of-force disparity across ten years and three presidencies points to structural factors rather than political leadership.

Part XI

The discretion effect

In Los Angeles, arrests are either non-discretionary (responding to a crime in progress) or discretionary (the officer decides whether to arrest). The scatter plot below places each racial group at its disparity index under both regimes. If discretion had no effect, all points would sit on the diagonal (y = x). The OLS regression line reveals the systematic pattern.

Each point = one racial group. Bubble size proportional to arrest count. Dotted diagonal = no discretion effect. Gold regression line: slope < 1.0 means discretion compresses disparity toward parity. Los Angeles arrests 2015. N = known-race subset.

The numbers: OLS regression on 126,854 Los Angeles arrests yields slope = 0.76 ± 0.07 (p = 0.001, t = 11.49, df = 3). The relationship is statistically significant and explains 97.8% of the variance (R² = 0.978). For every 1.0× of non-discretionary disparity, discretionary disparity rises by only 0.76×. Concretely: Black residents drop from 3.80× to 2.97× (Δ = −0.84×), the largest absolute shift of any group. White residents move from 0.53× to 0.69× (Δ = +0.16×). Hispanic residents barely change (0.96× → 1.01×). The compression is not random — it is a statistically significant linear pattern driven by the structure of charge categories, not by chance.
